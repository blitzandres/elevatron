# Abstract

We evaluate **Elevatron**, a conceptual launch-assist architecture in which a self-pinched, high-current beam — driven along the axis of an external ring/solenoid magnet array and conditioned by lasers — serves simultaneously as an electrical conductor and as a load-bearing "dynamic pillar." The proposal sits at the intersection of three mature research programs: the magnetic pinch (Bennett 1934; Z-pinch and MagLIF, Slutz 2010, Gomez 2014), beamed laser propulsion (Kantrowitz 1972; Myrabo lightcraft), and self-focusing relativistic beams for long-range power transfer (Greason).

We derive the Bennett equilibrium and show why the pinch confines a beam radially; we then test the dual-role conjecture quantitatively. The conductor role is sound — fully-ionized plasma is among the best current carriers known. The *structural* role is where the concept fails: the magnetic pressure achievable at safe field strengths is orders of magnitude below what is required to support a kilometre-scale column against gravity and atmospheric loading, and the column is intrinsically unstable to sausage (*m*=0) and kink (*m*=1) modes whose growth times are microseconds, not the seconds a launch corridor demands. Atmospheric breakdown and beam blooming compound the problem at sea level.

The honest verdict: the **megastructure** is not viable with foreseeable technology. The **physics is real and reproducible**, and the nearer-term cousins — beamed-energy propulsion, plasma-channel power transfer, and magnetic launch assist — are legitimate and active. We close with a graduate-level laboratory chapter giving safe, low-cost demonstrations of the pinch principle suitable for a teaching lab, and a fully-cited bibliography so each claim can be traced to source.
